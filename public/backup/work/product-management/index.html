Module 1

What Is Product Management?

Product management is one of the most misunderstood roles in tech because the job looks completely different depending on the company. But the core stays constant: a PM decides what gets built, in what order, and why — and is then accountable for whether the product succeeds in the market. What makes this hard is that PMs rarely have direct authority over anyone. They lead through influence, evidence, and communication.

The PM is a communications hub, not a boss

Nobody reports to a PM. Instead, a PM sits at the intersection of engineering, design, sales, support, marketing, and leadership — pulling information from all of them, making sense of it, and turning it into a coherent plan. The job has been described as "CEO of the product" but that analogy only works up to a point. CEOs have authority. PMs have to earn alignment through every interaction.

This means the most important skills are not technical — they are communication, prioritisation, and the ability to make good decisions under uncertainty without all the information you would want. Every day involves incomplete data, competing opinions, and limited resources. A PM's job is to make a call anyway, and to document why.

In an interview

When asked what a PM does, do not say "I make sure the team builds the right thing." Say: "A PM is accountable for the product's success without having direct authority over the people building it. That means defining the right problems to solve, making evidence-based prioritisation decisions, and communicating clearly enough that engineering, design, and the business are all aligned on why we are building what we are building."

Four things a PM does

Prioritise
Decide what gets built next and — equally important — what does not. Anyone can build a long list. The skill is in the cuts. Every item you say yes to is an implicit no to everything else in the backlog.
Research
Understand users, competitors, and data. This is ongoing, not a one-time project phase. Good PMs are always gathering signal from every available source — support tickets, analytics anomalies, competitor launches, industry news.
Present and communicate
Translate between audiences constantly. What engineering needs to hear differs from what a CEO needs. A PM who can only communicate in one register cannot do the job fully. Learn to adjust depth, framing, and format by audience.
Own outcomes
The PM is accountable for whether the product succeeds even when they did not build it personally. This is what separates PM from project management — a project manager delivers a defined project, a PM is responsible for whether that project worked.

Three types of PM

TypeWho you build forWhat the job is really like
Internal PMOther teams inside your companyLower stakes, smaller user base, deep tech stack exposure. The best first PM role — you learn the discipline without the pressure of a public product launch. Internal stakeholders are your primary feedback source.
B2B / SaaS PMOther businesses (clients)Small number of high-value users. Decisions often driven by revenue impact or client contract terms. Works closely with sales and account management. Risk: building for one loud client rather than the broader user base.
B2C PMThe general publicLargest user bases, highest uncertainty. Heavy reliance on data and testing. Requires strong vision and comfort with ambiguity. The most celebrated PM roles are B2C, but also the hardest entry point.

PM vs. Project Manager — know this cold

Product ManagerProject Manager
Responsible forA goal — defined by metrics and outcomesA delivery — defined by timeline and budget
The "what" isTheirs to defineAlready decided by someone else
Success meansThe product moves the right metricThe project ships on time and on budget

The nuance: every good PM still needs project management skill, because someone has to keep delivery on track. But a PM who only manages delivery without doing discovery and prioritisation is project management with a fancier title.

Module 2

Product Development Fundamentals

Before you can manage a product well, you need to understand the lifecycle of products in the market and the different philosophies for how to build them. These mental models explain why different companies build the way they do — and why a startup moves differently from an established enterprise.

The product lifecycle — four market phases

PhaseWhat is happeningPM implication
IntroductionJust launched. Little or no competition. Usually losing money because costs outrun revenue.Focus entirely on finding product-market fit. Speed of learning matters more than efficiency. Every assumption is unvalidated.
GrowthMarket acceptance happening. Sales rising. Relatively few competitors. Active improvement cycle.Scale what is working. Invest in retention. Watch for early competitors emerging. The roadmap starts to matter more here.
MaturitySales peak. Competitors flood in. Differentiation is harder. The market is crowded.Defend your position. Focus on segments you serve best. Innovation becomes more incremental. Cost discipline matters more.
DeclineMarket saturates. Sales fall. Technology shifts may make the product obsolete.Decide between sustaining, milking (harvest cash while reducing investment), or sunsetting (graceful wind-down rather than abrupt cutoff).

A practical tool for gauging lifecycle stage: Google Trends. Search interest trajectory gives a rough but real signal of whether a market is growing, peaking, or declining.

Development philosophies

Lean
Cut everything that is not yet proven to be necessary. Classic example: if you want to build a food-delivery app, lean thinking says you should do deliveries yourself first — on a bike, taking orders by phone — before building a platform. Only build once you have validated that people actually want the service. Core principle: do not build until you have validated interest.
Agile
Lean thinking applied to software development. Break work into small batches, ship iteratively, and adapt based on what you learn rather than trying to plan everything upfront. A music app using Agile builds the two to five most important features first, ships them, learns from usage, and decides what to build next. The key word is iterative.
Waterfall
Plan and specify all features completely before development starts, then build everything in one long sequential pass. Appropriate when a product cannot function in a partial state — operating systems, automotive engine control systems, skyscraper construction. When intermediate states are not usable or are unsafe, Waterfall is the right model. Not wrong — just suited to specific contexts.

Scrum vs. Kanban

How Scrum works

Fixed-length iterations called sprints — typically two weeks. Four ceremonies:

  • Sprint planning — pull top-priority items from the product backlog into a sprint backlog; discuss implementation
  • Daily standup — brief standing meeting: what did I do yesterday, what am I doing today, is anything blocking me
  • Sprint execution — team works tickets from To Do through In Progress to Done. Anything unfinished rolls into the next sprint
  • Sprint retrospective — what went well, what did not, what to try differently next sprint

Scrum gives a predictable rhythm and a clear way to measure velocity over time. The discipline of the sprint creates natural checkpoints for re-prioritisation.

How Kanban works

Cards move across board columns — To Do, In Progress, Done — with no fixed sprint cycle and no prescribed meeting cadence. One ongoing product backlog rather than a separate sprint backlog. Teams pull items when they have capacity.

More flexible, often used for support or operations work where volume is unpredictable. Trade-off: harder to forecast delivery timelines because there is no fixed cadence to measure velocity against.

Module 3

Ideas and User Needs

The PM is not the person who has all the ideas. This is one of the most important things to understand about the role. The PM gathers ideas from everywhere, evaluates them rigorously, and decides which are worth building. The skill is in the filtering and the decision-making, not in generating the ideas themselves.

Where PM ideas come from — E.M.U.C.

E.M.U.C. — Four sources of PM ideas
EmployeesCoworkers, management, yourself. Engineers notice technical debt that creates user problems. Support teams hear complaints daily. Sales teams hear what competitors are offering. Internal ideas are often the most actionable because they come with context.
MetricsProblems and inefficiencies surfaced by usage data. A drop in conversion, a spike in errors, a feature nobody uses — these are ideas waiting to be investigated. Data does not tell you what to build, but it tells you where to look.
UsersDirect feedback via forums, support tickets, email, social media, app store reviews, and user interviews. Primary source material — what real people say about their actual experience.
ClientsMainly relevant for B2B PMs. The organisations paying for the product have specific needs that may differ from end-user needs. Balance client requests against what is right for the product's long-term direction.

Getting to the real need — ask "Why?" three times

When someone makes a feature request, they are almost always describing a solution they have imagined rather than the underlying problem. Your job is to understand the problem before evaluating any solution.

The technique in practice

User asks for "a download button on the reports page."

  • Why do you want a download button? "So I can share the report with my manager."
  • Why do you need to share it that way? "My manager does not have access to the system."
  • Why does your manager not have access? "We only have five licences and they are all used by the team."

The real problem is licence allocation, not the absence of a download button. A download button is one solution. Sharing permissions, PDF export, or a read-only viewer role might be better. You only know that because you asked why.

The PM mindset: find a solution to a real problem, not fit a problem to a pre-chosen solution. Before building anything requested of you, ask: is this solving an actual underlying problem, and could this solution have unintended side effects?

Users vs. customers — different feedback for different purposes

The customer pays. The user uses it. In B2C they are usually the same person. In B2B they are often different people — and they give you fundamentally different types of feedback.

  • Customers (buyers) give feedback about features they want — the big-picture capabilities they need. They are thinking about the purchasing decision and renewal.
  • Users give feedback about the friction they hit while actually doing their work — the specific moments where the product gets in their way.

Over-indexing on customer feedback while under-investing in user experience creates products that get purchased but not used. You need both streams.

Module 4

Competitive and Market Analysis

Understanding your competitive landscape is not a one-time research exercise. It is a standing habit. A PM who does not know what competitors are shipping, how they are positioning, and what customers are saying about them is making product decisions with a significant blind spot.

Four types of competitors

TypeDefinitionHow to respond
DirectSame target customer, same problem. Customers explicitly choose between you.Be genuinely competitive. Track feature parity, pricing, and positioning closely. Have a clear and defensible reason someone should choose you.
IndirectSame problem, different approach, overlapping but not identical customer.Do not lose too much share. Monitor closely — indirect competitors often become direct as they expand product scope.
PotentialSame target customer, different problem currently.Make sure they cannot easily enter your space. If they already have your customer's trust, expanding is low-friction for them.
SubstituteSolves same core problem, very differently positioned.Be at least as good at solving the core problem. Substitutes steal customers who decide your category is not worth the switching cost.

Five criteria for evaluating any competitor

  1. Product core — who is building it and how strong is their team? Weak product leadership makes a competitor less threatening even with resources.
  2. User base size — larger bases mean easier press, better partnership leverage, and faster iteration loops. A large install base is a moat even when product quality is lower.
  3. Design quality — how strong is their product craft? A competitor who ships well-designed, usable products is more threatening than one who ships feature-dense but unusable ones.
  4. Brand — strong brands command loyalty, charge more, and get more benefit of the doubt when things go wrong. Slow to build, very hard to erode once established.
  5. Speed — how fast can they ship? Larger companies tend to slow over time. A well-funded startup with a small focused team can outship a large incumbent even on much smaller budgets.

Finding unknown competitors — three search techniques

  • Channel the user's complaint language — search the exact phrases a frustrated user would type about the problem you solve. This surfaces companies using similar language and individual complaints that lead to competitor mentions.
  • Site-restricted forum searches — search Reddit or Quora using your product's plain-language description. Real people asking for recommendations will name competitors you have never heard of.
  • Exact-phrase search — put quotation marks around how you would pitch your own product. Anyone ranking for the same language is competing for the same customer.

Monitoring competitors as a standing habit

Track three types of competitor events on an ongoing basis:

  • Funding rounds — more capital means more hiring, marketing, and shipping speed. Track via Crunchbase. A large raise is worth taking seriously as a signal they are about to accelerate.
  • Acquisitions — can strengthen a competitor's team and product capabilities overnight.
  • Feature launches — track via social listening tools and competitor blog subscriptions. Never be surprised by a competitor's launch.
Module 5

Customer Development

Customer development is a structured approach to maintaining a continuous feedback loop with your customers so you can form hypotheses, test them, and adapt. Formalised by Steve Blank. The core idea: you do not really know what customers want until you talk to them — and even then, what they say they want and what they will actually use are different things. Interviews reduce the gap between assumption and reality.

Four types of customer interviews

Exploratory
Open-ended discovery. No solution has been proposed yet. You are learning about the customer's world, their daily work, their frustrations, and their goals. The output is richer context before you begin to ideate. These come first.
Validation
Testing a specific hypothesis. You have a proposed direction and are asking: does this actually solve the problem? You may show a concept or prototype, watching for genuine excitement versus polite positivity. These come after you have formed a hypothesis.
Satisfaction
Checking how existing users feel about the current product. What is working? What frustrates them? What do they wish was different? Most useful when you have a live product and are deciding where to invest next.
Efficiency
Watching users actually use the product and observing where friction occurs. Unlike other types which rely on what people say, efficiency interviews capture what people do — which is often different. The gap between self-reported and actual behaviour is where the most useful insights live.

Running the interview correctly

The most important rule: the topic is their problem, not your solution. This is harder than it sounds because you almost certainly want to tell them about your idea. Resist it.

The rules of a good customer interview
  • Do not pitch your solution — customers cannot always tell you what they want, but they cannot hide what they need. Your job is to uncover the need.
  • Do not inject your own opinions — even body language counts. Nodding enthusiastically when they confirm your hypothesis biases the interview.
  • Let the conversation go off-script — the most valuable insights often come from tangents. If they start discussing a related frustration you had not anticipated, follow it.
  • Create psychological safety — avoid questions that make them feel judged. They should feel like they can say "this is terrible" without it being awkward.
  • Universal prompt when stuck — "That is interesting — tell me more." Works in every interview type without leading the witness.

Leave every interview understanding: who this customer is, what their habits are, and when, where, and why they would need your product.

The data diet — interviews are one source, not the only source

Interviews have two hard limits: they do not scale, and they are qualitative. A well-rounded PM triangulates across:

  • Internal feedback from support and sales teams
  • User-test data and usability study findings
  • Online and social feedback — app store reviews, forum posts
  • Product analytics — usage patterns, drop-off rates, feature adoption
  • Industry news and analyst reports
  • Competitor watching — what are they shipping and what are customers saying about it?

Never make a major product decision based on a single data source. Interviews tell you the why. Analytics tell you the what. You need both.

Module 6

Designing and Running Experiments — MVPs

An MVP is not a small version of your product. That is the most common misconception. An MVP is the smallest experiment you can run to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about whether your assumption is correct. The goal is not to build something — it is to learn something, as cheaply as possible. Eric Ries introduced this in The Lean Startup. The guiding mantra: fail fast.

The seven-step MVP process

Step by step
  1. Define your problem and solution hypothesis — what do you believe is true about the problem and the solution you are considering?
  2. Identify your assumptions — list everything that must be true for your idea to work. Frame each as: "In order for this to succeed, the following must be true..."
  3. Find the riskiest assumption — the one whose failure would sink the whole idea. Most often this is: "the customer actually has this problem."
  4. Build a specific, testable hypothesis around that riskiest assumption
  5. Establish your Minimum Criteria for Success (MCS) — the pre-defined bar that turns an ambiguous result into an actionable decision
  6. Execute the experiment — run it cleanly, do not change variables mid-experiment
  7. Evaluate and learn — compare result against MCS: proven, disproven, or needs another iteration?

Prioritising assumptions — the risk/difficulty matrix

Plot each assumption on two axes: how risky (how badly would this sink the idea if false?) and how hard to test. Test in this order:

  1. High risk / Low difficulty — test first. Cheap to test and could invalidate the whole idea.
  2. High risk / High difficulty — test next. They matter enough to invest in.
  3. Low risk / Low difficulty — test if time allows.
  4. Low risk / High difficulty — generally skip.

Writing a hypothesis — three levels of rigour

  • Basic: "We believe [target group] will [action] because [reason]."
  • Iterating on existing product: "If we [action], we believe [subject] will [outcome] because [reason]."
  • Most rigorous: "We believe [subject] has a [problem] because [reason]. If we [action], this [metric] will improve by [amount] within [timeframe]."

The more specific before the experiment, the more honest the evaluation of results will be after it.

Types of MVPs

TypeHow it worksBest for / notes
Email MVPPitch a fictional or upcoming feature via email; gauge reaction via clicks, replies, sign-upsFast, cheap, testable with a targeted segment. Pair with a landing page for best results.
Shadow buttonAdd a button that appears to link to a feature. Measure clicks. The feature does not exist.Quick raw interest signal. Always show a "thanks for your interest" message — never leave users confused.
404 / coming soonFeature appears in navigation but leads to a waitlist or error pageWorks well when you have an existing product. Amazon uses this frequently.
Explainer videoTutorial or sales video depicting the feature before it existsDropbox's pre-launch video drove 70,000 sign-ups before the product existed. Works for complex ideas hard to convey with text.
Fake landing pageSingle page pitching benefits with a call to action; measure conversion rateTesting demand for an entirely new offering. Conversion rate against a baseline gives you a real signal.
Concierge serviceManually deliver the outcome by hand, one customer at a time, no automationDeep qualitative learning. Cheap in money, expensive in your time. Worth it when insight outweighs efficiency.
Piecemeal MVPStitch together existing tools to fake the functionality (Zapier, IFTTT, no-code tools)Validate a workflow without custom engineering. Fast to assemble.
Wizard of OzLooks automated to the user; humans manually perform the steps behind the scenesZappos proved the model before building any infrastructure. Test service models before investing in automation.
Module 7

Conceptualising the Solution

Once you have validated a problem worth solving, you need to make solution concepts concrete enough for others to react to. This is where PMs work most closely with designers — but on small teams, PMs often need to produce rough concepts before a designer is involved. Key discipline: function before form. You are validating structure and flow, not aesthetics.

Wireframes, mockups, and prototypes

StageWhat it isFidelityWhen to use it
WireframeRough structural layout — where content blocks go, boxes and labels, no visual designLowEarliest stage. Validates page structure and information hierarchy before any design investment.
MockupStatic visual design with real colours, typography, and detail. Looks designed but not interactive.Medium-high (visual)After structure is validated. Gets feedback on visual design direction.
PrototypeClickable and interactive. Simulates how the product works well enough to reveal usability issues in the flow.High (interaction)Before development begins. Tests interaction patterns and user flows with real people.

Fix structural problems at the wireframe stage. Fix visual problems at the mockup stage. Fix interaction problems at the prototype stage. Each is cheaper to change than what comes after.

How to start sketching on paper

Five-step pen-and-paper wireframing method
  1. Draw a browser or app frame — a rectangle representing the screen boundary
  2. Add the logo and navigation — establish the global chrome before worrying about content
  3. Ask: what is the point of this page? What should users be able to do here?
  4. Sketch every function the page needs — search, filtering, a primary action, secondary actions, feedback states
  5. Look at comparable products you respect — not to copy, but to see how others have solved similar structural problems
Module 8

Metrics: Defining Success and Measuring Results

Product management is ultimately about moving numbers. Every major product decision you make should be connected to a metric you expect it to move, and after the fact you should be able to say whether it moved. This is what separates a PM who is doing the job from one who is just shipping features and hoping. As Peter Drucker put it: what gets measured gets managed.

What makes a metric good

  1. Understandable — everyone on the team can instantly grasp what it means and why it matters. If you have to explain it every time you mention it, it is not a good reporting metric.
  2. A rate or ratio, not a raw count — raw counts are easy to game or misread. "We have 10,000 users" tells you less than "35% of users who sign up make a second purchase within 30 days." Rates reveal actual behaviour.
  3. Correlated with the outcome you actually care about — if you are optimising a metric that does not connect to user or business value, you improve the metric while the product stagnates. Watch for vanity metrics.
  4. Changeable — something your team's actions can plausibly move. Tracking a metric you have no ability to influence wastes attention.

The HEART framework

Developed by Kerry Rodden at Google. Gives you a structured way to think about product health across five dimensions. Designed for ongoing reporting, not one-time research.

HEART — for each dimension define: Goal → Signal → Metric
H — HappinessHow satisfied are users? Measured via NPS, app store ratings, satisfaction surveys. Hard to measure accurately but important — emotional response affects retention and referral.
E — EngagementHow often and deeply are users interacting in the short term? Highly product-specific — meaningful engagement for a daily app differs from a quarterly tool. Define this specifically for your product.
A — AdoptionHow many of the people who could use the product are actually using it? Measures the gap between potential and actual user base. Low adoption despite high interest usually signals an onboarding or discovery problem.
R — RetentionHow many users keep coming back over time? The difference between retained users (consistently returning) and resurrected users (came back after being inactive) matters — resurrection is more expensive than retention.
T — Task successHow well can users complete the most valuable task the product is meant to support? Measured by completion rates, error rates, time-on-task. The most direct measure of whether the product works.
In an interview — how to measure success for a new feature

"I would start by defining which HEART dimensions matter most for this feature. For a feature intended to drive repeat usage, Retention and Task success are most relevant. I would define a goal for each, the signal I would measure to track progress toward that goal, and the specific metric I would report on over time. I would also set a pre-defined threshold for what success looks like before we launch, so the evaluation is honest rather than rationalised after the fact."

The AARRR / Pirate Metrics framework

Coined by Dave McClure. Describes the customer journey as a funnel. Especially common in SaaS and startup contexts. The name comes from the initials sounding like what a pirate says.

AARRR — five stages of the customer funnel
AcquisitionHow are users finding you? Which channels are driving traffic and sign-ups? Cost per acquisition by channel. Top of the funnel.
ActivationAre they having a meaningful first experience? Activation means they have taken the specific action that predicts future engagement — not just created an account, but done something that shows they "got it."
RetentionAre they coming back? Where most products leak. High acquisition and activation but low retention means you are filling a leaky bucket — replacing users as fast as you lose them.
ReferralAre happy users telling others? The most efficient growth channel because it is essentially free. NPS is the most common proxy for referral potential.
RevenueAre you making money from them? Lifetime value (LTV), cost per acquisition (CAC), monthly recurring revenue (MRR), and annual recurring revenue (ARR).

AARRR is most useful for identifying where your biggest problem is. Strong acquisition but weak retention means a product problem, not a marketing problem. Strong retention but weak acquisition means a distribution problem, not a product problem. The data tells you where to invest.

Module 9

Building the Product

Once you have validated a direction and have enough confidence to build, the PM's job shifts to delivery management. This does not mean becoming a project manager — it means ensuring the process is organised well enough that the team can build efficiently and what gets built matches what was designed and validated.

Epics and user stories

An epic is a grouping of features that together represent a larger initiative — typically anything taking longer than a single sprint. Not every epic results in a user-visible feature — some are internal or infrastructure work. An user story is a single unit of buildable functionality framed from the user's perspective: "As a [user type], I want [capability], so that [outcome]."

Acceptance criteria are the specific conditions that define a story as done. Written before development starts. The contract between PM and engineering. Without them, "done" is whatever the engineer thought you wanted.

Epic spec structure

Four sections of an epic spec sheet
  1. Introduction — summary of the feature and purpose, which metrics it targets, links to related documentation, marketing or legal notes, early wireframes
  2. Product requirements — what the feature must do, functionally, from the user's perspective. Not technical implementation — what it does and what constraints it operates under.
  3. Design requirements — filled collaboratively with a designer. Wireframes, mockups, interaction specifications.
  4. Engineering requirements — filled by engineers after discussion. Technical implementation approach, dependencies, estimated effort, technical constraints the PM should know about.

PM owns sections one and two. Designers own three. Engineers own four. But all are written collaboratively — the boundaries are about accountability, not exclusion.

Prioritisation methods

Assumption testing
Score each item on riskiness (1–10) and importance (1–10). Sum them and sort descending. Test riskiest and most important first. Most useful in early product stages when you are still validating fundamentals.
BUC method
Score Business benefit, User benefit, and Cost (each 1–10). Priority score = Business benefit + User benefit − Cost. Sort descending. High value to business and users but low cost to build should come first.
MoSCoW method
Bucket every item as Must / Should / Could / Would not. Always clear Musts before Shoulds, Shoulds before Coulds. Most useful for release planning when you have a fixed deadline and need to make scope decisions.
In an interview — how do you prioritise?

"I start by understanding the goal of the current sprint or release — what specific metric are we trying to move? Then I score candidate items against that goal by likely impact and cost. I also look at what assumptions we are still carrying that could undermine the plan, and I try to surface and test those early rather than building on top of unvalidated assumptions. When there is disagreement, I try to reframe the conversation around shared evidence rather than competing preferences."

Story points and velocity

Story points are a relative difficulty rating — not hours, but a team-consistent scale where a five-point story is twice as complex as a two-and-a-half-point story. Velocity is the total story points a team completes in a sprint. Tracking velocity over multiple sprints creates a reliable baseline for planning — after six sprints, you know approximately how much work your team can handle per sprint.

Roadmapping — honest about what roadmaps can do

Roadmaps are near-universally somewhat inaccurate. Software timelines shift, priorities change, unexpected problems arise. The value of roadmaps is directional alignment, not precision. An alternative many experienced PMs prefer: bucket work into Near-term / Mid-term / Long-term priority tiers rather than fixed dates. Keeps everyone aligned without over-promising timelines that will shift.

Module 10

Working with People and Stakeholders

The PM is the team's communications hub. The quality of your relationships with engineers, designers, and executives is directly correlated with the quality of what gets built. A PM who produces great strategy but cannot get engineering to care about it will not ship good products. This is not soft skills as an afterthought — it is the primary mechanism through which product work happens.

Working with engineers

How to build a strong relationship with engineering
  • Treat spec quality as your responsibility — if something goes wrong because of ambiguous requirements, that is on the PM. Write precise acceptance criteria. Ambiguity in a spec becomes a bug in the product.
  • Have a point of view on the longer-term direction — engineers think about architecture. If you describe only the next increment without context for the longer-term direction, they may make architectural decisions that make the future state much harder to reach.
  • Do the groundwork yourself where possible — checking logs, pulling basic data, investigating an anomaly before asking an engineer to investigate it. Engineers resent being used as data-retrieval services. Learning basic SQL is one of the most practical things a PM can do.
  • Take technical debt seriously — engineers are aware of shortcuts that will cost more time later. Dismissing their concerns erodes trust and leads to slower shipping as the codebase degrades.
  • Invite their input on the how — do not hand down fully-baked specs with zero room for engineering perspective. The best product decisions happen when PM, design, and engineering collaborate on the solution, not when PM specifies and engineering executes.

Working with designers

  • Give designers real creative latitude — do not come with a pre-formed solution. Say "users need to complete this task without losing their place" not "I need a modal with a form." Let them solve the problem.
  • Ask for their actual product opinions — designers interact with the product at a level of detail that reveals problems PMs often miss. Their opinions about what is wrong are valuable data.
  • Always lead with the user problem — let solutions come second. The output is almost always better when designers arrive at solutions rather than being handed specifications to execute.
  • Share all relevant data with them — analytics, research findings, competitor benchmarking. A designer working from an incomplete brief makes worse decisions.

Working with executives

  • Be brief — two bullet points beat two paragraphs. Executive time is genuinely scarce. If you cannot explain the key point in one minute, you do not understand it well enough yet.
  • Frame in terms of business impact — revenue, metrics, risk, competitive position. Not implementation detail, not technical approach, not design rationale.
  • Adapt to their communication style — some want dashboards, some want narrative memos, some want a five-minute verbal summary. Find out which one works and use it every time.
  • Always explain why, not just what — executives who understand your reasoning trust your future decisions. Those who only see outputs constantly relitigate decisions.
Module 11

Technology for Product Managers

You do not need to code to be a great PM. But you need to understand how technology works well enough to have informed conversations with engineers, assess the feasibility of product ideas, and understand the implications of technical decisions. A PM who cannot discuss the tech stack with credibility loses influence with the engineering team over time.

Front end, back end, and tech stacks

Front end
Everything the user directly sees and interacts with. Three core languages: HTML (structure and content), CSS (visual styling — colours, fonts, spacing), and JavaScript (interactivity — what happens when you click something, form validation, dynamic content).
Back end
Servers, databases, and the logic away from the user's screen. When you submit a form, the back end receives it, processes it, stores it in a database, and sends back a response. Common technologies: MySQL (relational database), Amazon Web Services (cloud infrastructure).
Tech stack
The full set of technologies — front end through back end — used to build a product. Knowing your company's tech stack helps you understand what is easy to build, what is hard, and what requires significant investment.

APIs — the connective tissue of modern software

An API (Application Programming Interface) acts as a messenger: it takes a request from one system, tells another system what is being asked for, and returns the result. APIs are how modern software is built — rather than building everything from scratch, systems communicate with each other through APIs.

  • Public APIs let outside developers pull data from a company's services
  • Private APIs are internal-only — the same company's different products talking to each other

Important PM implications: APIs have rate limits, they can fail (build fallback behaviour), and integrating with a third-party API creates a dependency. If that API changes or goes down, your product is affected.

Technical skills worth developing

  • Basic SQL — lets you pull your own data from a company's database without waiting on an engineer. Even knowing enough to write a simple SELECT query makes you more self-sufficient and credible with engineering teams.
  • Version control basics — knowing what Git is, how branches and pull requests work, makes you a more effective collaborator with engineering even if you never write code yourself.
  • Relational vs. non-relational databases — relational databases (MySQL, PostgreSQL) store data in structured tables with relationships. Non-relational (MongoDB) store data more flexibly. The choice affects what kinds of queries are fast and what data structures are natural.
Module 12

Preparing Yourself for the PM Job

There is no university major in product management. People enter the role from engineering, design, business analysis, marketing, customer success, and every adjacent discipline. What matters is not where you come from — it is whether you can demonstrate the mindset and skills the role requires. That demonstration starts before you have the title.

Turning existing experience into PM experience

Reflect on any past instance where you did the following — these are all PM-relevant even without a PM title:

  1. Had to prioritise among multiple competing tasks, alone or with a team
  2. Talked with users or customers of a product or internal tool, even informally
  3. Gave feedback or reported bugs on any product — internal or external
  4. Interacted with a product team — helping gather data or giving input on direction
  5. Coordinated work across multiple teams toward a shared outcome
  6. Improved the efficiency of an existing process — identified a problem, proposed a solution, implemented it

Any of these can become a resume bullet and interview talking point. The key is framing the experience in terms of outcomes and impact, not just activities.

Building a portfolio via a side project

If your current job offers nothing PM-adjacent, a self-directed side project is the standard alternative path. Document a genuine PM-style process: identify a user problem, generate and prototype a solution, and articulate how you would change an existing product to better serve its users. Strong formats: design a feature improvement for a product you use regularly, conduct five user interviews about a specific frustration and write up your findings and proposed solutions, or build a rough prototype of a product idea and test it with real potential users.

Module 13

Looking for a Job in Product Management

Where to look — in order of effectiveness

  1. Inside your own company first — lowest-friction path. If a PM team exists internally, express interest, learn what they are working on, and volunteer for relevant tasks. An internal transfer requires no one to take a risk on an unknown quantity — you are already known.
  2. Networking — meet people already in product management through meetups, industry events, or LinkedIn. Referrals dramatically increase your chance of an interview because they carry implicit credibility.
  3. Job boards and communities — startup-focused boards, tech community "who's hiring" threads, PM-focused newsletters with job postings alongside content.

Vetting the opportunity before investing in applying

  1. Does the company actually understand what a PM does? Some organisations conflate product and project management. If the job description is mostly "managing timelines" and "coordinating deliverables" without mention of user research, prioritisation, or product strategy, you may be looking at project management with a fancier title.
  2. Research the existing product team and leadership — look at their LinkedIn, blog posts, talks. Working under someone with genuine product experience accelerates your development substantially. Working under someone who does not understand the discipline is a slow way to stagnate.
Module 14

Getting the Job

Resumes — quantify everything

Product management is about moving metrics. Your resume language should reflect that. The most important principle: quantify everything you possibly can.

WeakStrong
"I managed marketing campaigns.""Increased the number of simultaneous campaigns managed by 20% over the course of the year."
"I worked with the development team to improve the website.""Led a cross-functional team to reduce page load time by 40%, improving conversion rate by 12%."
"I gathered customer feedback and used it to guide decisions.""Conducted 30 user interviews across three customer segments, identifying a retention problem that led to a feature change reducing monthly churn by 15%."

PM interviews — demonstrating structured thinking

How to approach PM interview questions
  • Ask clarifying questions first — jumping straight to an answer without probing for context is a common and visible mistake. "Before I answer, can I ask a few clarifying questions?" signals exactly the right instinct.
  • There is rarely a single correct answer — the interviewer is evaluating your reasoning process, not your conclusion. Show your thinking, not just your answer.
  • Always show your work — how you arrived at a conclusion, why you discarded other options, and what you would want to learn more about before committing.
  • Treat every interview as practice — note the questions you got, review the ones you fumbled, prepare better answers before the next one.

The demonstration project — the insider tip that almost no candidates use

How to build a PM demonstration project
  1. During the interview process, ask genuine questions about why they are hiring, what the role would actually work on, their market and customers, and technical context
  2. Independently research the company's product — use it, read reviews, look at competitors, talk to potential users if you can
  3. Identify a specific opportunity: a user problem, a metric you think could be improved, a competitor advantage not being addressed
  4. Put together a short document covering: your research, your proposed approach, relevant technical constraints, rough wireframes, a hypothetical A/B test design, competitive analysis, and any customer feedback you gathered
  5. Send it as a thank-you follow-up after an interview

This demonstrates initiative, customer empathy, analytical thinking, and communication skills — the four things every PM interview is actually trying to evaluate. Most candidates never do it. The ones who do are remembered.

Module 15

After You Have Got the Job — The First 90 Days

The first few months in a new PM role are the most important. You are in listening mode, building credibility by understanding before you act. The biggest mistake new PMs make is arriving with answers before they understand the questions. Your job in the first 90 days is to learn, not to prove you already know everything.

Six things to do in your first month

First 30 days playbook
  1. Schedule a 1:1 with each team member — understand their individual goals, current frustrations, and what they think the most important problems are. Keep detailed notes. Patterns across these conversations tell you a lot about team health and the product.
  2. Meet the lead engineer and get a full technical walkthrough — understand what technologies are in use, where the biggest technical debt is, and what engineering considers the most important problems. Their perspective is invaluable and often underrepresented in product planning.
  3. Start talking to actual users as quickly as possible — book five to ten customer interviews in your first month. Consider shadowing customer support for a few hours — you will learn more about real user pain points in an afternoon than in weeks of reading documentation.
  4. Read everything internal you can access — past epics, release notes, sales decks, internal presentations. The institutional knowledge in documents is usually richer than what you will be briefed on in onboarding.
  5. Study the existing metrics — what is currently tracked, what the team plans to track next, and where there are gaps in measurement. Understanding the measurement infrastructure tells you what the team values and where they are flying blind.
  6. Meet with your manager and align on expectations — what does success look like at 30, 60, and 90 days? What are their biggest priorities? Clarify early rather than assuming.
Quick reference

Glossary

MVPMinimum Viable Product — the smallest experiment that yields validated learning about whether an assumption is correct. Not a small product. An experiment designed to produce learning at minimum cost.
MCSMinimum Criteria for Success — the pre-defined bar an experiment must clear to be deemed a success. Set before the experiment, not after seeing the results.
KPIKey Performance Indicator — a metric used to judge whether the product or initiative is succeeding. Good KPIs are understandable, are rates or ratios, correlate with actual outcomes, and can be moved by your team's actions.
EpicA grouping of features that together represent a larger initiative — anything taking longer than a single sprint to complete.
User story"As a [user type], I want [capability], so that [outcome]." The basic unit of agile delivery planning.
Acceptance criteriaThe specific conditions that define a user story as done. The contract between PM and engineering written before development starts.
SprintA fixed-length development iteration — typically two weeks — used in Scrum.
VelocityTotal story points a team completes in a sprint. Used to forecast how much work can be done in future sprints.
Story pointsA relative difficulty rating system. Team-consistent — a five-point story is twice as complex as a two-and-a-half-point story, even if no one can say exactly how many hours either will take.
HEARTHappiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task success — a framework for measuring product health across five dimensions.
AARRRAcquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue — the pirate metrics funnel describing the customer journey from first contact to revenue.
Feature tableA comparison matrix of your product versus competitors across key dimensions. Forces explicit reasoning about differentiation.
Jobs-to-be-doneA framework for thinking about products in terms of the underlying job customers hire them to do — not demographics. Introduced by Clay Christensen.
Tech stackThe complete set of technologies — front end through back end — used to build a product.
APIApplication Programming Interface — a messenger system that lets one piece of software request and receive data from another.
Technical debtShortcuts made during development that will cost more time to fix later. Engineers track this carefully; accumulated debt slows future development.
E.M.U.C.Employees, Metrics, Users, Clients — the four sources of ideas a PM draws from.
MoSCoWMust / Should / Could / Would not — a prioritisation method for bucketing backlog items by urgency and importance.
BUC methodBusiness benefit + User benefit − Cost — a scoring method for prioritising backlog items across three dimensions.
TAMTotal Addressable Market — the total revenue opportunity if you captured 100% of the market. Used in top-down market sizing.
LTVLifetime Value — total revenue a customer generates over the entire duration of their relationship with your product.
CACCustomer Acquisition Cost — the average cost of acquiring one new customer. Should be significantly lower than LTV for a sustainable business.
MRR / ARRMonthly / Annual Recurring Revenue — predictable revenue from subscription customers. Core SaaS metrics.
