Work/Ways of Working

Ways of working for a UX team.

The most common failure mode for a design team is not a lack of talent. It is a lack of structure. Work arrives without a clear pathway. Designers make isolated decisions that conflict with each other. Knowledge sits in individual heads and disappears when someone leaves. The team works hard and still delivers inconsistently, because the system around the work has not been built with the same care as the work itself.

An operating model solves this. Not by adding process for its own sake, but by making the right decisions easier to make consistently: who needs to be involved at each stage, what pathway a piece of work should take, when to escalate, how decisions get documented, and how the team reviews work collectively rather than in isolation.

Why Ways of Working Matter

The team is the system. And like any system, it works best when it is designed.

Managing a team of senior designers across multiple product squads , alongside service designers, content managers, and business analysts , means that the manager cannot be in every conversation. The team needs to be able to make good decisions independently, know when to escalate, and collaborate effectively without waiting for direction on every piece of work.

That requires explicit structure. Not because senior designers cannot think for themselves, but because without shared expectations, every designer develops their own interpretation of what good looks like, when to involve others, and what level of fidelity is appropriate for a given stage of work. Over time, that divergence costs more in rework and alignment than any individual process overhead would have.

The ways of working framework below defines three delivery pathways, each matched to a different type of work. Work that needs new discovery before any design begins takes the dual-track path. Work where the problem is understood takes the solution discovery path. Work the design system already solves takes the existing components path, meaning no new design work, just governance and guidance.

The pathway decision is made collectively in the Studio session at the start of every piece of work, not by the manager alone, and not by the individual designer. That collective ownership of the process is what makes it actually used rather than documented and ignored.

The Studio Model

The Studio is a standing weekly session where the full team brings work at any stage: early sketches, mid-process decisions, near-final designs, for collective critique. It is the single most important practice for maintaining consistent quality across a distributed team.

Without a shared critique space, quality depends entirely on whether individual designers happen to have the right conversations with the right people at the right time. The Studio, the team develops a shared sense of what good looks like, informed by the collective experience of UX, service design, content, and business analysis all in the same room.

The Studio also functions as a safety net. When a team member went on leave mid-project, the rest of the team already understood the work because it had been through the Studio. The system absorbed the disruption without anything being dropped. That is the actual test of whether a practice is real or decorative.

AI Orchestration Layer

As the team reduced in size, the question became how to maintain quality and knowledge access without adding headcount. Individual designers in product squads were physically distributed across the organisation. Knowledge was fragmented across tools. A designer starting a new piece of work had no reliable way to know what research existed for that product area, what the design system already covered, or what decisions had been documented previously.

The orchestration layer connected these sources. A Slackbot connected to the design system, journey maps, research repository, live products, and internal documentation, so a designer could ask a question in the channel they were already working in and get an answer synthesised from all available sources. The knowledge layer also connected to WCAG accessibility guidelines, solution architecture standards, brand guidelines, and legal requirements, meaning the team could surface constraints from across the organisation rather than working in isolation from the broader standards they were expected to design against.
