Section 1

Introduction to Design Thinking

Design thinking for problem solving

Five stages from the Stanford d.school: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test. Humans at the centre throughout. Problem statements have four parts: the problem, the persona, the outcome, and how to measure it. No solutions in the problem statement. Iterative — repeat as needed.

Section 2

Product Definition, Ideation, and Frameworks

Design thinking for product development

Design fits in the first three phases: discovery, define, design. Works ahead of development — sometimes by a few sprints. Poor planning causes usability issues, poor metrics, and limited scalability. In agile: design requirements established, tested, then handed off as epics broken into stories.

Screenshot: design in agile delivery diagram

Understand customer behaviors and goals

Qualitative plus quantitative data together give the full picture. Analytics shows what is happening. Interviews reveal why. Survey first to segment roles, then interviews, then contextual inquiry. Spend 20 minutes after each interview capturing takeaways while fresh.

Identify and synthesize customer insights

Affinity mapping: one insight per sticky note, cluster into themes, let themes emerge from the data. Synthesis reveals research gaps as much as patterns. If themes are not clear, go back and interview more people.

Screenshot: affinity mapping example from course

Develop customer personas

Research-based illustrative models. Components: name, occupation, goals, tasks, pain points, opportunities. Provisional personas work without research — best guesses supplemented by data. Primary use: alignment across the team.

Screenshot: persona template from course

Visualize customer journey maps

Capture phases, activities, pain points, emotions, quotes, opportunities, metrics. Primary use: identifying gaps and pain points. If information is missing, go back and talk to users.

Section 3

Uncovering and Identifying Business Problems

User centered product requirements

Requirements derived from user research rather than business assumptions. Iterative feedback loop with real users. Traditional project management is business-first and linear. User-centred requirements ensure people will actually want to use the product.

Ideation for user experience

Divergent process first — generate as many ideas as possible without constraints, time-boxed to 15-20 minutes. Low fidelity sketches to move fast. Then converge — pick strongest ideas and add detail. Do not constrain yourself too early.

Plan user flows

Map every decision point and connection before designing screens. Site map tracks pages and views — user flow adds decisions and logic. Both are needed. Flows reveal gaps before detailed design begins.

Screenshot: user flow diagram from course

Gathering feedback

Stay low fidelity so feedback shapes the design before you invest in it. Sketches are enough. Ask open-ended questions, avoid leading questions. You just need enough to get reactions. Fail fast: get wrong answers early when they are cheap to fix.

Section 4

Usability and Design Paradigms

Information architecture

How people find and move through a product. A good IA is invisible. Global navigation is accessible everywhere. Page navigation organises content within a defined area. Site map tracks pages and views — does not capture decisions or logic.

Screenshot: site map or IA diagram from course

Cognitive load and Gestalt principles

Cognitive load: mental effort required to process a page. Gestalt principles are how the brain naturally organises visual information — design with them to reduce cognitive load.

ProximityElements close together are perceived as related. White space creates grouping without visible borders.
SimilarityElements that look alike are perceived as functioning alike.
ClosureThe brain completes incomplete shapes. A line graph does not need a solid line to read as continuous.
EnclosureA bounded area is perceived as a group. Cards work because of this.

Screenshot: Gestalt principles diagrams from course

Usability factors

Design patternsRepeatable solutions. Reduce cognitive load through familiarity. Must suit specific context.
IAPoor IA makes things unfindable regardless of visual design quality.
FlowIf sequence does not match expectations, usability suffers even with well-designed screens.
Visual designColour, typography, iconography, layout. Affects both desirability and usability.

Fitts's law, affordance, and guiding principles

Fitts's Law: time to reach a target depends on size and distance. Interactive elements must be large enough to tap accurately, especially on mobile.

AffordanceAppearance suggests function. Shadows signal a button is pressable.
ConsistencySame patterns throughout. Users learn once. Core argument for design systems.
Mental modelsUsers bring expectations from other products. Match them for immediate intuitiveness.
FeedbackEvery action gets a response — loading states, hover states, confirmations.
Error preventionDesign so errors are unlikely. When they occur, users can reverse them.

Section 5

User Interface Design and Prototyping

Fundamentals of user interface design

Visual hierarchy: position, size, weight, colour. Important things go first. Without hierarchy users cannot tell what to look at first.

TypographySize and weight create reading order. Same weight everywhere makes nothing stand out.
ColourDraw focus or de-emphasise. Never colour alone — support with text and icons. Too much colour increases cognitive load.
Grid12-column grid standard for web. White space between columns separates elements.
White spaceActive design element. Reinforces grouping and reduces cognitive load.

UI components and elements

Buttons, forms, sliders, drawers — building blocks of interfaces. Design systems collect these with documentation: what they are, when to use, when not to, error states, anatomy, context. A Figma component maps to a coded component — shared source of truth.

Screenshot: component documentation from course

Low fidelity design with Figma

Paper Wireframe Kit from Figma Community — intentionally rough so people focus on interactions, not visuals. Goal: get the flow and navigation structure right before layout decisions. Navigation first, content second.

Screenshot: paper wireframe kit in Figma from course

Interactive prototyping with Figma

Connect screens with triggers. Smart Animate detects matching layers and animates differences — reinforces feedback principle. Four screens with a few connections is enough to test with users.

Quick reference

Psychology principles

Hick's LawMore choices = longer to decide. Reduce options at key decision points.
Fitts's LawTarget acquisition time depends on size and distance. Make interactive elements large enough.
Miller's LawWorking memory holds 7 plus or minus 2 items. Chunk information into groups of 5 to 9.
Jakob's LawUsers spend most time on other sites. Design to meet existing expectations.
Cognitive LoadMental effort has a limit. Good design reduces extraneous load so users focus on the task.
ProximityElements close together are perceived as related.
SimilarityElements that look alike are perceived as functioning alike.
ClosureThe brain completes incomplete shapes.
EnclosureBounded areas are perceived as groups. Cards work because of this.
Von RestorffThe different element is most remembered. Primary CTA buttons use a different colour for this reason.
Zeigarnik EffectIncomplete tasks are remembered better. Progress bars create tension that motivates completion.
Peak-End RuleExperiences judged by peak and end. The confirmation screen matters as much as the form.
AffordanceAppearance suggests function. Shadows signal a button is pressable.
Aesthetic-UsabilityAttractive interfaces are perceived as easier to use, even when they are not.
