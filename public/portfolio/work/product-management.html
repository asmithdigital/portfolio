Module 1

What Is Product Management?

A PM is a communications hub — not a people manager. They pull input from engineering, design, sales, support, leadership, and users, and turn it into a coherent plan. Accountable for whether the product succeeds, even without direct authority over the people building it.

Core PM functions

Prioritiser
Deciding what gets built next, and what does not.
Researcher
Understanding users, competitors, and data.
Presenter
Communicating plans and rationale to different audiences.
Owner of outcomes
Accountable for the product's success even without direct authority.

Three types of PM

TypeWho you build forKey characteristics
Internal PMOther teams inside your companyLower risk, deep tech stack knowledge, great first PM role
B2B / SaaS PMOther companies (clients)Revenue-driven, client contract deadlines, close sales collaboration
B2C PMGeneral publicLarge user bases, high uncertainty, heavy testing and data reliance

PM vs. Project Manager

A Product Manager is responsible for a goal measured by metrics — how the goal is reached is largely up to them. A Project Manager is responsible for delivering a defined project against a fixed timeline, with the "what" already decided. Good PMs still need project management skill, but their real job is defining the right goal, not just hitting a deadline.

Module 2

Product Development Fundamentals

Product lifecycle — 4 phases

PhaseWhat is happening
IntroductionJust launched, little competition, usually losing money
GrowthMarket acceptance, rising sales, few competitors, active improvement
MaturitySales peak, competitors flood in, harder to differentiate
DeclineMarket saturates, sales fall, product may be phased out

Development philosophies

Lean
Ruthlessly cut unnecessary work until you are certain it is needed. Do not build until you have validated interest.
Agile
Break work into small batches, ship them one at a time, adapt based on feedback rather than planning everything upfront.
Waterfall
Plan and build all features together in one long sequential pass. Right when a product cannot function with partial capability.

Scrum vs Kanban

Scrum uses fixed-length sprints (typically 2 weeks) with sprint planning, daily standups, and retrospectives. Work flows from product backlog to sprint backlog. Kanban is looser — cards move across columns with no fixed sprint cycle and no prescribed meetings. More flexible but harder to forecast velocity.

Module 3

Ideas and User Needs

Where PM ideas come from — E.M.U.C.

EEmployees — coworkers, management, yourself
MMetrics — problems surfaced by usage data
UUsers — direct feedback via forums, email, social media
CClients — mainly relevant for B2B PMs

The PM is not "the idea person." Their real job is gathering ideas from everywhere and deciding what to build and when.

Getting to the real need

People ask for a solution they have imagined, not what they actually need. Before building anything, ask: is this solving an actual underlying problem? Could this have unintended side effects? Technique: ask "Why?" three times until you reach the real pain point behind a request.

Users vs. customers

The customer pays. The user uses it. They are not always the same person. Customers give feedback about desired features. Users give feedback about usability and technical friction. You need both feedback streams.

Module 4

Competitive and Market Analysis

Four types of competitors

TypeDefinitionHow to respond
DirectSame target customer, same problemBe genuinely competitive
IndirectSame problem, different approach, overlapping customerDo not lose too much share to them
PotentialSame target customer, different problemMake sure they cannot easily start solving your problem
SubstituteSolves same core problem, very differently positionedBe at least as good

Five criteria for evaluating a competitor

  1. The product core — who is building it and how good is their team?
  2. User base size — bigger bases mean easier press and faster iteration loops
  3. Design quality — how strong is their product craft?
  4. Brand — strong brands command loyalty and can charge more
  5. Speed — how fast can they ship changes?

Feature tables

A comparison matrix — competitors along one axis, features or factors along the other. Forces you to be explicit about why a customer should pick you. Useful before launch (check competitiveness) and after (evaluate a single proposed feature against what rivals already offer).

Module 5

Customer Development

A framework for maintaining a continuous, iterative feedback loop with customers. PMs use it for risk mitigation and opportunity recognition. Three-phase cycle: validation, first version development, iteration.

Four types of customer interviews

Exploratory
Open-ended discovery — understand their world before any solution is proposed.
Validation
Test a specific hypothesis — does your proposed solution match their real need?
Satisfaction
Check how existing users feel about the current product.
Efficiency
Watch them use the product and observe where friction occurs.

Running the interview correctly

The topic is their problem, not your solution. Do not pitch. Do not inject your own opinions. Let the conversation go off-script — that is often where new ideas emerge. Universal prompt when stuck: "That is interesting — tell me more." Aim to leave understanding who they are, their habits, and when and why they need your product.

The data diet

Interviews are one source. A well-rounded PM triangulates across: internal feedback, user-test data, online and social feedback, analytics, industry news, market trends, and competitor-watching. Never make a major call from a single source.

Module 6

Designing and Running Experiments (MVPs)

MVP = Minimum Viable Product. The version of a product that lets a team collect the maximum validated learning about customers for the least effort. The point is not to build a small product — it is to run a scientific-style experiment as cheaply as possible. Guiding mantra: fail fast.

Seven-step MVP process

  1. Define your problem and solution hypothesis
  2. Identify assumptions and find the riskiest one
  3. Build a specific, testable hypothesis around that assumption
  4. Establish your Minimum Criteria for Success (MCS)
  5. Choose which MVP type and strategy to run
  6. Execute the experiment
  7. Evaluate and learn from the results

Risk and difficulty — what to test first

Plot assumptions on two axes: how risky and how hard to test. Test High risk / Low difficulty first. Then High risk / High difficulty. Low risk / Low difficulty if time allows. Low risk / High difficulty — generally skip.

Hypothesis formats

Basic: "We believe [target group] will [action] because [reason]."
Iterating on existing: "If we [action], we believe [subject] will [outcome] because [reason]."
Most rigorous: "We believe [subject] has a [problem] because [reason]. If we [action], this [metric] will improve."

Types of MVPs

TypeHow it works
Email MVPPitch a fictional feature via email and gauge reaction
Shadow buttonButton that appears to link to a new feature — measure clicks
404 / coming soon pageFeature appears live but leads to a waitlist page
Explainer videoTutorial or sales-style video depicting a not-yet-real feature
Fake landing pageSingle page pitching benefits, measure conversion
Concierge serviceManually deliver the outcome by hand for a small group
Piecemeal MVPStitch together existing off-the-shelf tools to fake functionality
Wizard of OzLooks automated to the user, but humans manually perform the steps behind the scenes
Module 7

Conceptualising the Solution

StageWhat it isFidelity
WireframeRough structural layout — where content blocks goLow
MockupStatic visual design with real colours and detailMedium-high
PrototypeClickable and interactive — reveals usability issues in the flowHigh

Guiding principle at the wireframe stage: function over form. You are validating structure and flow, not aesthetics. Start with a browser or app frame, add logo and navigation, then ask: what is the point of this page and what should users be able to do here?

Module 8

Metrics: Defining Success and Measuring Results

What makes a metric good

  1. Understandable — everyone on the team can grasp what it means
  2. A rate or ratio — not just a raw count
  3. Correlated with the outcome you actually care about
  4. Changeable — something your team's actions can plausibly move

The HEART framework

For each dimension define: Goal → Signal → Metric
HHappiness — how happy is the user?
EEngagement — how engaged are they in the short term?
AAdoption — how many interested people have actually tried the product?
RRetention — how many keep coming back long term?
TTask success — how well can users complete the most valuable task?

The AARRR / Pirate Metrics framework

AAcquisition — how are users finding you?
AActivation — are they signing up and meaningfully engaging?
RRetention — are they coming back?
RReferral — are they happy enough to tell others?
RRevenue — are you making money from them?

Five metric families

  1. Growth and Activation — new users, activated users
  2. Retention — consistently returning vs. resurrected users
  3. Engagement — how often and deeply users interact
  4. User happiness — NPS, ratings, complaint volume
  5. Revenue — LTV, CAC, MRR, ARR
Module 9

Building the Product

Epics and user stories

An epic is a grouping of features that represent a larger initiative — typically anything taking longer than a single sprint. A user story is a single unit of buildable functionality: "As a [user], I want [something], so that [outcome]." Acceptance criteria define when a story is done.

Prioritisation methods

Assumption testing
Score each item's riskiness (1–10) and importance (1–10), sum them, sort descending. Test riskiest and most important first.
BUC method
Score Business benefit, User benefit, and Cost (each 1–10). Sum the two benefit scores, subtract cost, sort descending.
MoSCoW method
Bucket each item as Must / Should / Could / Would not this time. Always clear the Musts first.

Roadmapping

Roadmaps are always somewhat inaccurate but valuable as directional guides. An alternative to date-based roadmaps: bucket work into Near-term / Mid-term / Long-term priority tiers. Keeps everyone aligned without over-promising specific delivery dates.

Module 10

Working with People and Stakeholders

Working with engineers

  • Treat spec quality as your responsibility — ambiguous requirements are on the PM, not the engineer
  • Have a real point of view on where a feature is headed longer-term, not just the next increment
  • Do the groundwork yourself where possible — checking logs, pulling data — rather than making engineers do PM-adjacent legwork
  • Take technical debt seriously — engineers are aware of shortcuts that will cost time later
  • Invite their ideas and feedback on the how — do not hand down fully-baked specs with zero room for input

Working with designers

  • Give designers real creative latitude — do not dictate the visual solution
  • Ask for their actual product opinions, not just execution
  • Always lead with the user problem and let solutions come second
  • Share all relevant data with them, not just a brief

Working with executives

  • Be brief — two bullet points beat two paragraphs
  • Frame everything in terms of business impact — revenue, metrics, risk — not implementation detail
  • Adapt to their preferred communication style
  • Always explain why you landed on a solution, not just what the solution is
Module 11

Technology for Product Managers

You do not need to code, but understanding how common technologies work gives you: a realistic sense of what is actually buildable, credibility and rapport with engineers, and an understanding of the real implications of the decisions you are asking for.

Key concepts

Front end
Everything the user directly sees and interacts with. HTML (structure), CSS (styling), JavaScript (interactivity).
Back end
Servers, databases, and the logic away from the user's screen. Common: MySQL, cloud infrastructure like Amazon S3.
API
A messenger that takes a request, tells the underlying system what is being asked for, and returns the result. Public APIs let outside developers pull data. Private APIs are internal only.
Tech stack
The complete set of technologies — front end through back end — that make up a given app.

Learning basic SQL lets a PM pull their own data from a company's database without waiting on an engineer.

Module 12

Preparing Yourself for the PM Job

There is no PM degree. Candidates demonstrate the mindset and skills through existing experience. Reflect on any past instance where you:

  1. Had to prioritise among multiple competing tasks
  2. Talked with users of a product or internal tool
  3. Gave feedback or reported bugs on any product
  4. Interacted with a product team — helping gather data or giving feedback
  5. Coordinated with multiple teams on something
  6. Improved the efficiency of an existing process

Any of these can become resume bullets and interview talking points, even without a formal PM title.

Module 13

Looking for a Job in Product Management

Where to look

  1. Inside your own company first — lowest-friction path
  2. Networking — meet people in PM through local events for referrals and insider information
  3. Job boards and PM-focused newsletters

Vetting the opportunity

Does the company actually understand what a PM does? Some conflate product and project management. Research the existing product team and leadership — working under someone with genuine experience accelerates your own learning substantially.

Module 14

Getting the Job

Resumes

If you lack formal PM experience, reframe existing experience in PM-relevant terms and quantify everything. Product management is fundamentally about moving metrics.

Weak: "I managed marketing campaigns."
Strong: "Increased the number of simultaneous campaigns managed by 20% over the course of the year."

Answering PM interview questions

  • There is rarely a single correct answer — the interviewer is evaluating your reasoning process
  • Always show your work: how you arrived at a conclusion, why, and what alternatives you considered
  • Before answering a case question, ask clarifying questions — jumping straight to an answer without probing is a common and visible mistake
  • Treat every interview as practice for the next one

Standing out: build a demonstration project

During the interview process, research the company's product, market, technology, and competitors independently. Put together a mini "how I would approach this" project — wireframes, hypothetical A/B tests, competitive analysis, customer feedback. Send it as a thank-you follow-up. This demonstrates initiative that most candidates never take the time to show.

Module 15

After You Have Got the Job

  1. Schedule 1:1s with each team member — understand their goals and current challenges
  2. Meet the lead engineer — get a walkthrough of the technologies and the biggest technical challenges
  3. Start talking to actual users — build direct understanding of what matters to them
  4. Read everything internal you can access — past epics, mobile release notes, sales decks, presentations
  5. Study the existing metrics — what is currently tracked and what the team plans to track next
  6. Meet with your manager — clarify expectations and understand their goals and pain points
